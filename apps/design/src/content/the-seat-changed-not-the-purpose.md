There is a specific kind of gravity that attaches itself to the seat in student politics. We call it a seat, but it's rarely about the chair itself. It is about the symbol of status that comes with it. Within higher education, these titles often become one of the primary currencies among students. They become a way to signal influence before anyone has actually demonstrated what they can contribute.

I remember that back in 2016, I had applied for the role of Minister of ICT in the Student Executive Board of Telkom University, and I walked into the interview expecting a standard evaluation of my skills. Instead, the student president told me that my CV was overkill. He said I could easily defeat any other candidate for the position, and he can just give the seat on the spot. But, he also offered me a staff role in the Ministry of Cabinet Resource.

You may think that it sounds like a demotion. However, the reasoning he provided may change your perspective. Well, it changed mine after all. The staff role in Cabinet Resource was essentially a steering committee. It was where the actual observation and advising happened. More importantly, he proposed a different path as the first Media Coordinator for Aliansi BEM SI. This was a national-wide organization and there was a critical urgency there that a local ministry title could not match. I said yes, not because I wanted a cooler seat, but because the critical urgency it held.

## The Utility of the Side-Step

This experience taught me early on that there is a difference between holding a position and holding influence. A title is a label, but influence is the ability to affect the system. In the case of the Aliansi BEM SI, the role was less about prestige and more about the urgency of the mission. I understood the ins and outs of the media landscape and knew that the national organization needed someone who could actually execute. The weight came from the fact that we were voicing people's aspiration across the country. The risks were higher. A single misstep in communication could be amplified by thousands, while pressure from external power structures was a constant presence. The "prestige" of a local ministerial seat felt hollow compared with the actual responsibility of safeguarding the voices of a national movement.

And the logic of a steering committee is fundamentally different from the logic of who actually executes. A minister manages a team and carries out a predefined mandate, while a steering staff member can operate across organizational boundaries, observing how things work, providing insights, and supporting its members when necessary. The prestige may be lower, but the leverage is higher. I realized that by letting go of the seat, I gained a more direct line to the actual decision-making processes.

About a semester later, the student president approached me again. This time, he offered me the position of Minister of Cabinet Resource because the previous minister, who was from our same year, had completed his studies and graduated. He expected me to take the seat as a natural progression. When I said no to the offer, he was genuinely confused. I laughed, and he thought I was trying to mess with him, but I told him I was being serious. I rejected the seat because that was never my goal. Not even the Minister of ICT position had ever been a goal in itself. I was driven by the purpose and the urgency to make a difference in that field. I wanted to be effective, and I had no interest in treating student organizations as places to accumulate titles for my CV, especially when the work behind those titles wasn't something I genuinely cared about.

This was my first lesson in designing a professional path based on utility rather than status. I stopped looking at the organizational chart as a ladder to climb and started seeing it as a map of influence. Most people treat titles as a way to build a portfolio, collecting labels to signal a certain level of success to the world. I realized that this is a fragile way to operate. I stopped asking what the highest title I could get was, and started asking if my current arsenal was sufficient for the mission at hand. I preferred to be the person who could actually solve the problem over the person who simply held the title of the one who should solve it.

## Between Competence and the Illusion of Seniority

By 2017, the dynamics shifted. I had joined the Students Legislative Council and found myself in a strange position. I was the most senior student still active in the student body while the Speaker of the House was three years younger than me. Most of my peers from the same year had already graduated, leaving me in what I call the "offside year", where you are just exceeding the normal study window of your degree.

Because of this seniority, the Speaker proposed that I take the seat of the Ethics Committee. He reasoned that the internal body was struggling with ethics and that the other students would respect a senior. I refused and I did so jokingly at first. But the real reason was simpler. I had only been a legislator for a few months and I had no idea how the legislative process actually worked.

I refused to take a position of such gravity without the necessary competence. It was a moment of friction where the prestige of the seat clashed with my own integrity. I knew I understood the requirements of the national alliance because that was my domain, but I was a novice in the legislative body. I did not have the specific tools required to navigate that environment, and taking the seat just for the status would have been a mistake. I saw that the respect the Speaker expected was not actually respect, but rather a form of hesitation. The other students were not looking for a guide, they were simply afraid of the weight of the role. I refused to be a figurehead who held a title without the functional capacity to back it up.

This taught me that seniority is a liability if it is not backed by a deep understanding of the current system. The most senior person is often the most exposed when things go wrong, particularly if they take on leadership without first learning the mechanics. I chose to remain a novice in the eyes of the council rather than be a figurehead who failed the people he was supposed to guide. I think there is a specific kind of arrogance in accepting a role of authority before you have mastered the basic operations of the environment itself.

I wasn't trying to follow a particular route through either organization. I knew what I wanted to do, even when I didn't know which position would allow me to do it best. I was willing to change direction when another opportunity brought me closer to that purpose, but I also had to recognize when I wasn't equipped to take on a responsibility. The position could change, but I still had to be honest about what I could actually deliver.

## The Irony of Oversight

I became the Vice-Speaker of the House in 2018, and found myself overseeing the very committee I had previously turned down. What an irony, i thought to myself. I hadn't planned for that trajectory, but by then I had developed a better understanding of the council and was ready to take on a different level of responsibility. Looking back, I realized that my previous refusal had been a logical choice. Had I taken the seat back then, I would have been a person with no understanding of how to do my role. By taking the time to learn how the system worked, I was eventually able to oversee the committee with the knowledge the role required. This experience reinforced my belief that saying no can sometimes be a strategic tool.

This process showed me that professional growth is not a linear climb up a ladder of titles. Instead, it is a process of recognizing and mapping your abilities to the most impactful point of the system. Sometimes that means taking a step sideways or even a step down in title to gain the leverage you actually need. Becoming Vice-Speaker wasn't the culmination of a title I had been pursuing. It was another responsibility I could take on after developing a better understanding of the organization and its needs.

It makes me wonder about the people who spend their life fighting for those seats. If you are willing to push others aside for a title despite being incapable of performing the role at anything beyond a mediocre level, what is the actual goal? I struggle to understand the logic of chasing a position you cannot effectively fill. Is it a lust for power that makes them disregard the responsibilities, or are they so mediocre in their judgment that they cannot even think far enough ahead to recognize their own inadequacy?

This is not just a student problem. In the real world, we see people climbing corporate ladders or seeking political office for the same reasons. They chase the seat of power without ever developing the capacity to wield it. They mistake their position for their capability, and in doing so, they become fragile. When the environment changes, the person who relies on their seat is the first to be left behind because they have forgotten how to adapt. I suspect that the most valuable thing you can do in any environment is to figure out the difference between having a seat and having influence. Influence is earned through utility and the ability to adapt to a problem, while a seat is often just a piece of furniture that anchors you in place.
